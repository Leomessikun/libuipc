# Could we finish this project the way RL-100 did?

Date: 2026-09-21. Branch `research/expo-ft-dressing`. Assessment against this
project's measured numbers; no new run. Companion to
[the EXPO-FT fit](2026-09-21-expo-ft-fit.md), which answers a different question.

## 1. First, the two papers are not the same thing

- [Real-Time EXPO-FT](https://pd-perry.github.io/real-time-expo-ft/) is about
  **inference latency**: a large VLA is slow, the world moves while it thinks, so a
  learned edit policy adapts its chunk using the observation available at execution
  time.
- [RL-100](https://lei-kun.github.io/RL-100/) (Kun Lei, Huanyu Li, Dongjie Yu,
  Zhenyu Wei, Lingxiao Guo, Zhennan Jiang, Ziyu Wang, Shiyu Liang, Huazhe Xu;
  Science Robotics) is about **real-world reliability**: a three-stage pipeline —
  imitation, then offline RL, then online RL — reported to reach 100 % success
  across seven manipulation tasks, 95.0 % average under perturbation, 92.5 %
  zero-shot across four kinds of change, and consistent 100 % after **about 200
  episodes of on-policy rollouts**. It runs at 30 Hz, camera-limited, and chooses
  single-step control or action chunking per task; a consistency-model policy is
  what makes the RL policy fast enough to execute.

"Real-time" is EXPO-FT's word; RL-100's is "real-world". For a simulator project
the distinction matters: we have no latency problem to solve, and we do have a
reliability problem.

## 2. The budget question answers itself

RL-100's online stage is the expensive part of its recipe in the real world —
200 on-policy episodes is a lot of robot and human time. In our units it is not
expensive at all:

| | transitions | our cost at .0610 s per transition |
|---|---:|---:|
| 200 on-policy episodes of 300 decisions | 60,000 | **1.02 h** |
| 400 episodes | 120,000 | 2.03 h |
| 1,000 episodes | 300,000 | 5.08 h |
| one Stage 0 arm, for comparison | 450,000 simulated | 7.6 h |
| the scripted expert's 25 demonstrations | — | 12.2 min |

So the stage RL-100 says converts a good policy into a reliable one costs us about
**one hour of one workstation**, less than a seventh of what a single Stage 0 arm is
spending right now. Budget is not what stands between this project and that recipe.

## 3. What does stand in the way: the precondition, not the polish

RL-100 polishes a base that already works — imitation is "the sponge base", online
RL is "the cherry on top". Every number this project has measured says our base is
not yet at that stage:

| what | measured here |
|---|---|
| scripted expert on the 25 cells | 15/25 at peak, 11/25 final, **6/25** once the grasp limit is applied |
| a fitted student from reset, on the development cell | **0/4**, where the controller it was fitted from is 4/4 |
| best RL evaluation ever recorded on those cells | 7/25, one snapshot |
| the demonstrations available | 25 expert episodes plus 12 reconstructed, all with `priv_dim` 0 |
| the reward | contains no term for the criterion that decides success |

A polish stage takes a policy from mostly-right to reliably-right. Ours is not
mostly-right, and two of the reasons are measured and cheap to remove rather than
deep: 4 of the 25 scripted "failures" are complete dressings that the progress
metric reads as 1.00 -> 0.00 once the opening passes the shoulder, and all ten of
the real failures are the same elbow stall, not ten different problems.

## 4. So: yes, with this order

The recipe is the right shape and its budget suits us. The sequencing has to
respect what we measured, which means two cheap repairs *before* the three stages,
not after:

1. **Fix the success rule** past the shoulder and truncate overshoot episodes at
   their peak. Free; it changes the expert's score without changing its behaviour.
2. **Repair the teacher at the elbow** — stall detection, back-off, lateral
   centring — and re-score on the 25 cells. About 12 minutes of simulator per
   pass, and it is the difference between demonstrations that end in the failure
   we are trying to learn away and demonstrations that do not.
3. **Imitation** on the repaired demonstrations, with the relation-goal interface
   if Stage 1 shows it closes the execution gap.
4. **Offline RL** on those plus the existing replay, under the Stage 0 constrained
   objective, because RL-100's tasks have no absorbing constraint and ours does.
5. **Online RL**, about 200 episodes, roughly an hour. Report per-cell success on
   held-out garments and bodies, not an average.

Points 1 and 2 are the ones this project keeps deferring, and they are the ones
RL-100's recipe assumes are already done.

## 5. What it would and would not let us claim

It would let us finish: a dressing policy trained on one workstation, reported as
grasp-valid completion on held-out cells with total hours attached. That is a
systems result and a useful one.

It would **not** be a new RL algorithm. Imitation, then offline RL, then online RL
on an expressive policy is exactly the pipeline RL-100 published, in Science
Robotics, and copying it is not a contribution — the same screen this project
already applied to search-plus-fitting, teacher-student distillation and reset
curricula applies here. The consistency-model half is about executing fast on real
hardware at 30 Hz, which is a problem we will only have on the Stretch 3, not in
simulation.

So the honest choice is between two different papers, and the owner should make it
deliberately: **finish the task** with RL-100's recipe and write a systems result,
or **keep the algorithmic line** — the constrained objective and the query
allocation — and use the recipe only as the baseline it is. The two are compatible
in that order; they are not compatible as one blurred claim.

Verification note: the numbers above come from RL-100's own project page. It
publishes no per-stage data volumes, no algorithm names per stage and no limitation
section, so the pipeline's details should be read from the paper before any of this
is treated as a specification.
